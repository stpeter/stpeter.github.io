Destiny Calling

2026-09-05

In previous journal entries, I've explored the lesser-known ideas of Spanish philosopher José Ortega y Gasset. Here I'd like to extend that series by looking at his insights into what we might call personal destiny, which can be found in his 1949 essay "In Search of Goethe from Within" (printed in the expanded 1968 edition of The Dehumanization of Art and Other Essays on Art, Culture, and Literature).

Ortega proposes that every person represents a certain design for living: there is no such thing as human life in the abstract, but only the particular lives of particular human beings. This design for living - a vital program, a destiny, a vocation, a task, a calling, an ergon, an entelechy, a potential, a plan of being (etc.) - is not something you freely choose, he says, but instead is a pre-ordained potential that you can choose to realize or not. If you choose to realize it, then you live authentically; if not (e.g., because you substitute some other vital program for the one to which you are destined), then you live inauthentically. A human life is something that has to make itself - it is not a thing that fits a general pattern, but is inherently a task of self-discovery. There is no pre-existing solution to life, because at root your life consists in the problem of itself ("life is preoccupation with itself"). The essential question for each individual person is not "what am I?" but "who am I?" Thus "living is precisely the inexorable necessity to make oneself determinate, to enter into an exclusive destiny, to accept it - that is, to resolve to be it."

I admit that I'm not sure what to make of Ortega's proposal. On the one hand, I tend to recoil from any form of essentialism and I'm skeptical about the notion of the true self; on the other hand, we're all familiar with the reality of people living second-hand lives in which they succumb to external pressure instead of living by their own lights (e.g., by giving up their true love to marry someone their parents prefer, or by pursuing a conventional career instead of working at something they really love to do). Furthermore, what seems destined in one phase of life might not seem destined later on. And then there are all the practicalities of life: "do what you love and the money will follow" has always struck me as pure folly.

And yet, I do feel there are things I was born for. I even feel a kind of destiny about the people I care for most. To be sure, there's a great deal of contingency involved in who you meet in life, but with certain special people things feel so deeply right that the feelings I have for them seem to be a matter of destiny.

Eudemian Ethics I.3

2026-08-24

(Previous: Eudemian Ethics I.2.)

It would be a waste of effort to look into every opinion that anyone has seen fit to have about it, because many opinions occur to children or to those who are ill or out of their minds that no one with any sense would puzzle over. What they need is not reasoning but on the one hand the changes that come with maturity or on the other hand correction, whether medical or social, since medication is no less a corrective than punishment.

Likewise with the opinions of the multitude, for they speak in an offhand way about everything, and especially about this. Indeed on this topic it is only the opinions of the wise that must be considered (for it would be strange to bring reason to bear on those who look not to reason but to experience). Since there are impasses in each field of study, clearly they exist also in relation to living best and the greatest way of life. Thus it is a beautiful thing to put these opinions to the test, for by cross-examining a claimant we spell out the opposing account.

Moreover it is worth the effort to not let such things escape our notice, especially those to which every inquiry must direct itself: the things that make it possible to share in living well and living beautifully (if it is too brazen to say "living blessedly") and that give humane people hope of attaining it. For if living beautifully consists in things which come about by chance or by nature then most people could not hope for it (since in that case they couldn't acquire it by taking care of things that are up to them or their own affairs). On the other hand, if it consists in a certain quality in oneself and one's actions, then what's good will be more common and more divine: more common because it will be possible for more people to share in it, more divine because fulfillment could be attained by those who impart a certain quality to themselves and their actions.

Eudemian Ethics I.2

2026-08-24

(Previous: Eudemian Ethics I.1.)

Focusing now on these things, everyone capable of living as guided by their own commitments must set up some target of living beautifully - whether honor or reputation or wealth or culture - toward which they will look in doing all of these actions, because not organizing one's way of life around something that makes it complete is a sign of much foolishness. First and foremost, then, one should determine for oneself, neither hastily nor sluggishly, what of ours living well consists in, and the thing without which human beings cannot attain it. For that without which it is impossible to be healthy is not the same thing as being healthy, and something similar holds in many other cases; thus living beautifully is not the same as that without which it is impossible to live beautifully. Some of these things are not specific to health or to living but are common to everything, as it were, both traits and actions (for instance, without our share of breathing or being awake or movement nothing either good or bad would exist for us); whereas others are more in the nature of each thing, and these must not escape our notice (for the aforementioned things are not inherent to vigor in the same way as eating meat or taking a walk after dinner). These are the grounds for disagreement about fulfillment, both what it is and what brings it into being; for some consider things without which one cannot be fulfilled to be part of fulfillment.

The Best Way to Grieve Is to Give

2026-08-22

While corresponding recently with a friend of Elisa's about the celebration of life I'm planning, I happened to formulate the following phrase: "the best way to grieve is to give". Although I doubt that's a universal truth, it's been true to my own experience since Elisa's passing in February. For the first few months everything looked very dark, but slowly I started to engage more with the world - volunteering in my community, fulfilling my responsibilities as president of my neighborhood association, helping friends and family members in need, and making a few beautiful commitments that I won't talk about here because they are private acts of deep personal significance. Many sources of solace have helped me cope, but I've found that giving love to people I care about is especially effective. It's all too easy to ruminate about my situation and wallow in my emotions; by contrast, giving has not only gotten me out of my own head but has also helped me redefine my identity and gain greater self-knowledge about who I am and who I want to be in the final third of my life. Thus I plan to keep on giving as I venture forward into my future.

Thinking in Six Directions

2026-08-18

Here's a way of looking at life that I've wanted to write about for quite a while. If you envision your life as a long walk on a path (perhaps with a friend or two), six directions provide you with orientation: forward along the path before you, backward from whence you came, upward from the ground beneath your feet, downward from the sky above you, inward from the people at your side, and outward from yourself into the world. Exploring the metaphors, I think of these six directions in the following ways:

  1. The forward direction is all about your aims, goals, hopes, and dreams for the future. These can extend beyond your own life to that of your children and descendants, the influence you might have on the course of humanity (howsoever slight), etc.
  2. The backward direction encompasses your life history, your childhood, your family, your ancestry, and really the whole history of humanity before you were born.
  3. The direction upward from the earth beneath you does justice to physicality, earthiness, your animal nature and innate drives, biology, the forces of evolution, the chthonic gods, etc.
  4. The direction downward from the sky above you embraces your ideals, philosophy, religion, the sky gods, etc.
  5. The direction inward from the people around you takes in sociality, love, friendship, brotherhood, community, the formative and sometimes deformative influences upon you of living in human society, and so on.
  6. The direction from your personhood outward is bound up with everything that shines forth from your soul and everything that you place out into the world: the love you give in all its many forms, your creativity, the thoughts you share, and so on.

Although I find this schema to be helpful in my own thinking about life, I haven't put much effort into defining the directions with precision because I'm not sure how valuable such an exercise would be, and in any case the directions strike me as more suggestive than definitive. As always, your mileage may vary...

Eudemian Ethics I.1

2026-08-17

In the sacred precinct on the island of Delos, he who declared his opinion, distinguishing what's good and what's beautiful and what's enjoyable as not all existing in the same thing, inscribed the following verse at the entryway to the temple of Leto:

Most beautiful is doing right,
The greatest good is having health,
But that which brings the greatest joy
Is to obtain your heart's desire.

However, we do not agree with him. For fulfillment, being the best and most beautiful of all things, also brings the greatest joy.

Many of the matters requiring our attention about each thing and each nature introduce impasses and must be looked into. Some of these contribute only to our knowing, whereas others relate to getting things and taking action. Regarding those which are the concern of that passionate practice of sagacity which solely cultivates our attention, when the right occasion presents itself we must state what is inherent to that inquiry. But foremost we must look into what it is to live well and how to attain it.

Do all who happen to be called fulfilled become that way by nature, as with those who are tall or short or differ in complexion? Or do they learn to be that way, so that fulfillment is a kind of understanding? Or do they become that way through a sort of training (for many qualities exist in human beings not by nature or from learning but through enculturation, worthless ones if worthlessly enculturated, worthy ones if worthily enculturated)? Or is it none of those ways, but in one or the other of these: either breathed upon us by some divinity as if inspired (as happens to people possessed by some nymph or god) or by luck (for many say that fulfillment and good fortune are the same thing). That its presence exists in people by all of these or some of them or one of them is not unclear, for pretty much everything that happens comes down to these sources, and even all the actions that result from thinking things through can be brought under the heading of understanding.

Finding fulfillment - that is, living blessedly and beautifully - consists especially in three things which are thought to be most preferable: some people say the greatest good is wisdom, some excellence, some enjoyment. Yet some contest about the importance of these for fulfillment, saying one of them contributes more to it than another: some say that wisdom contributes as a greater good than excellence, others that excellence is greater than wisdom, yet others that enjoyment is even greater than both of those. Moreover, some say that a fulfilled life consists in all of these, some two, some a certain one.
