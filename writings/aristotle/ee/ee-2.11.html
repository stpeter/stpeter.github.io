Aristotle's Eudemian Ethics

Book II, Chapter 11

Translated by Peter Saint-Andre

Having set forth these definitions, we can say whether excellence prevents one's commitments from going astray and makes one's completion straight-and-true, with the result that one makes commitments for the sake of what one should - or whether, as some say, it does this for one's reasoning. But the latter is one by self-restraint, which pevents one's reasoning from being ruined; and self-restraint is something different from excellence. We must speak of these matters later on, since those who believe that excellence makes reasoning straight-and-true do so on the grounds that this is what self-restraint accomplishes, and self-restraint is something that's praised.

But let us proceed to another impasse. It is possible for the target to be straight-and-true but to go astray in what contributes to hitting the target; on the other hand, it is possible to go astray with regard to the target but to be correct about what will accomplish it; and both are also possible. Does excellence make the target straight-and-true or does it make what contributes to hitting the target straight-and-true? We posit that it does this for the target, since that is not the result of reasoning or argument but is set down as the starting point. For a doctor doesn't look into whether one ought to be healthy or not, but whether to walk or not; nor does a physical trainer look into whether one ought to be in good shape or not, but whether to wrestle or not; and likewise no other pursuit looks into that which brings it to completion. Just as in pursuits directed to attention the hypotheses are the starting points, so also in pursuits directed to making the starting point and hypothesis is that which brings them to completion. Here "if this is what being healthy is, then such-and-such must be provided if being healthy is to be present" is just like, there, "if a trianble is equal to two right angles, then such-and-such follows." The starting point of thinking is that which brings the pursuit to completion, whereas taking action begins where thinking leaves off.

Therefore, if everything straight-and-true is gounded in either thinking or excellence, it follows that if what makes the completion straight-and-true isn't thinking it must be excellence (although this is not the case for what conduces to completion). And the completion is the aim, i.e., that for the sake of which: for every commitment is a commitment both to something and for something. Now the aim, i.e., that for the sake of which, is the middle way; this is grounded in excellence, which makes a commitment to the aim that for the sake of which (indeed, what we commit to is not the activity but the aim). However, success in doing what must be done to accomplish the aim is a matter for another capacity: what excellence does is ground the commitment to what makes the completion straight-and-true.

This is why we assess the quality of a person from their commitments - not from what they do but from that for the sake of which they do it. Likewise deficiency makes its commitments for the sake of what's opposite. If it is up to someone to do what's beautiful and not to do what's ugly but they do just the opposite, manifestly that person is not an outstanding human being. Thus deficiency must be done willingly, as also excellence is, for there is no necessity to take corrupted actions. This is why we find fault with deficiency and praise excellence, for if it were done unwillingly we wouldn't find fault with what's bad and ugly, nor would we praise what's good - for these are done willingly.

Moreover, it is with an eye to the commitment rather than the deeds that we praise or blame, even though the activity of excellence is more preferable, for people can be compelled to take worthless actions but no one is compelled to make worthless commitments. Furthermore it's because it is not easy to discren the quality of a person's commitments that we find it necessary to assess them based on their deeds. Activity is more preferable, but commiment is more worthy of praise. This follows from what we have set forth, and also agrees with what's evident.
