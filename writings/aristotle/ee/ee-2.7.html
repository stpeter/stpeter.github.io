Aristotle's Eudemian Ethics

Book II, Chapter 7

Translated by Peter Saint-Andre

Thus we have to grasp what is the willing and the unwilling, and what commitment is. First we must inquire into the willing and the unwilling, since they set the bounds of excellence and deficiency. Indeed they would seem to exist in one of three ways: as directed by yearning or commitment or thinking, since the willing is directed by one of these and the unwilling goes against one of these. However, yearning itself is divisible into three varieties - wishing, wanting, needing - so we must distinguish among these, and first of all what is directed by need.

It would seem that everything which accord with need is done willingly, for everything done unwillingly seems to be compellsed and what is compelled is painful, as is everything that people are forced to do or undergo, just as Evenus said:

For all things forced, by nature bring distress.
Thus if something is painful it is compelled, and if compelled it is painful. Everything that goes against need is painful (for need yearns for what is pleasant), hence so is what's compelled and unwilling. Therefore whatever is directed by need is done willingingly, since these are opposites.

Moreover, every form of corruption makes a person more liable to do wrong, and self-indulgence seems to be a form of corruption. The self-indulgent person is the sort who takes action in a way that is directed by need and goes against reasoning, and is self-indulgent whenever activated in that direction. Because wrongdoing is done willingly, the self-indulgent person will do wrong in taking action as directed by need. Therefore the self-indulgent person takes action willingly, and what is directed by need is done willingly. (For it would be strange if self-indulgent people became more liable to do right.)

From these considerations it would seem that what is directed by need is done willingly; however, the opposite conclusion would seem to hold from the following. Everything that one does willingly is done as one wishes and everything dones as one wishes is done willingly. Yet no one wishes for what they consider to be bad. However, the self-indulgent does not do as they wish, for to be self-indulgent is to go against what one considers best by taking action due to need. Therefore it will happen that the same person takes action both willingly and unwillingly at the same time, for that is impossible.

Moreover, the self-restrained person will do right, since self-restraint is more of an excellence than self-indulgence and excellence makes people more liable to do right. Further, a person is self-restrained when they take actions that go against needing and accord with reasoning. Thus if doing right is done willingly, as doing wrong is (for both of these seem to be done willingly, and if one of them is then so must the other), and what goes against needing is done willingly, then the same person will do the same thing both willingly and unwillingly at the same time.

The same argument holds for wanting. For there seem to be both self-indulgence and self-restraing with respect to wanting just as with needing: whatgoes against wanting is painful and being compelled is a form of restraint, so that what is compelled is done unwillingly and everything that accords with wanting would be done willingly. And it seems Heraclitus had the power of wanting in view when he said that mastering it is painful: "it is hard", he said, "to fight against wanting, for it buys its way at the price of life." If it is impossible for the same person to do the same things willingly and unwillingly at the same time and in respect to the same aspect of the situation, then what accords with wishing would be done more willingly than what accords with needing or wanting. Take this as proof: we do many things willingly without feeling an impulse or need to do so.

It remains, therefore, to look into whether what is wished for is the same as what is done willingly. But this too appears to be impossible, for we have assumed (and it seems to us to be the case) that corruption of character makes a person more liable to do wrong and that self-indulgence is a sort of corruption, whereas the opposite would result [if what is wished for were the same as what is done willingly]. For no one wishes for things they consider to be bad, but does them after having become self-indulgence. If then doing wrong is something done willingly and what is done willingly accords with wishing, then once a person has become self-indulgent they could not do wrong, but instead would become more liable to do right than they were before becoming self-indulgent. But that too is impossible.

Evidently, then, what's done willingly is not action that accords with yearning, nor is what's done unwillingly action that goes against yearning.
