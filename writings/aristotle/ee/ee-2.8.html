Aristotle's Eudemian Ethics

Book II, Chapter 8

Translated by Peter Saint-Andre

Furthermore, that it is in turn not action that accords with commitment is manifest from what follows. What accords with wishing had been shown to be done not unwillingly, but rather everything which is wished for is also done willingly. (In fact all that has been shown is that it is possible to take action willingly without wishing - we take action in the moment on manythings we wish for, but no one makes a commitment to anything in the moment.)

If what's done willingly must be one of these three things - what accords with yearning or commitment or thinking - and if it is not two of these, it remains that what's done willingly consists in somehow taking action with thought. Leading our account a small step further along, let us complete the process of bounding off the willing and the unwilling. For it seems that what is done under compulsion or not under compulsion has some likeness to what we have been talking about, since we say that what is compelled is done unwillingly and that everything done unwillingly is compelled. Thus we first need to look into compulsion, both what it is and how it is related to the willing and the unwilling.

With regard to taking action, it seems that what is compelled and necessitated, and compulsion and necessity, are in opposition to persuasiosn and to what is done willingly. In general we also speak of what is compelled and necessitated with regard to non-living things; for instance we say that it is by compulsion and necessity that a stone is borne upwards or fire downwards. By contrast, when something is dorne along in a way that accords with its nature and its own internal impulse, we don't say that it does so by compulsion nor yet that it does so willingly. Although when it is borne along in a way that goes against these we say it does so by compulsion, the opposite has no name.

Likewise with living things and with animals, we see that often they do or undergo things by compulsion, when they are changed by something external in a way that goes against their own internal impulse. In non-living things the source of change is a straightforward matter, but in living things it is more complex, since yearning and thinking are not always in harmony. Hense in the other animals what is compelled is a straightforward matter, just as it is with non-living things, since they experience no conflict between thinking and yearning, but live only by yearning. Yet both of these are present in human beings, at least one they have reached the age at which we give an account of their actions. We don't say that a child takes action, not a beast, but only one who takes action due to reasoning.

Everything compelled seems to be painful, and no one delights in doing something under compulsion. That's why there is so much controvery about people who are self-restrained and people who are self-indulgent, for each of them takes action under conflicting internal impulses. Thus it's said that under compulsion the self-restrained person drags himself away from the pleasure he craves (for it pains him to drag himself away in resistance to his yearnings). By contrast, it's said that under compulsion the self-indulgent person goes against his reasoning, yet he seems to expereince less pain, since craving is for what's pleasant and he heeds that with delight, so that the self-indulgent person is more willing and not compelled in that he is not pained. Moreover, persuasion is the opposite of compulsion and necessity: the self-restrained person drives in the direction he has been persuaded to go and proceeds not under compulsion but willingly, whereas craving drives a person without persuading since it has no share in thought.

We have stated that these alone seem to act both willingly and under compulsion, grounded in a certain likeness to the compulsion we speak of in relation to non-living things. However, if we add what was specified in the previous distinction then what we just said is resolved, for when a thing is changed or kept as it was by something external that goes against its own internal impulse then we say it is under compulsion, but when it is not then we say that it is not under compulsion. But within the self-restrained person and the self-indulgent person the impulse that drives him forward is present internally (for he has both). Thus neither of them is under compulsion, but due to these considerations takes action willingly and not by necessity. For when an external source either hinders or changes something against its impulse we say there is necessity, just as if one were to grab someone's hand and use it to hit someone against their wishes and desires - but when the source is internal, the action is not compelled.

Moreover, pleasure and pain are present in both cases. The person exercising self-restraint experiences pain in the moment of acting against his cravings, yet delights in the expectation of benefiting from his future health or even in his immediate health. The self-indulgent person delights in undulging in whatever he happens to crave, but is pained by the expectation of future distress since he knows that he does something bad.

Thus we have reason to say that each of these people acts under compulsion and that due to yearning or reasoning each of them sometimes takes action unwillingly; since yearning and reasoning are two separate things, each one can overmaster the other. Hence people ascribe it to all of personhood when they see such a thing within personhood. Indeed it's possible to say this of an aspect, but the whole personhood of the self-indulgent person or the self-restrained person takes action willingly and neither of these people does so under compulsion (even though some aspect of them might) since by nature we have both aspects.

Also, reasoning is by nature one of the sources of taking action, which will be present when a person't development is allowed to proceed and is not stunted, and so too is needing, which is present and follows straightaway from birth. Indeed, roughly speaking we bound off what's by nature from these two things: what follows straightaway from birth in everyone and what comes about in us when our development is allowed to go straight forward, for instance grey hair and old age and other such things. Thus in a way each of these people takes action not in accord with nature, although plain-and-simple each does take action in accord with nature, albeit not the same nature.

These, then, are the impasses about the self-restrained person and the self-indulgent person: whether one or both of them take action under compulsion (so that they do so either not willingly or at the same time both under compulsion and willingly) and, if what's compelled is done unwillingly, whether they take action both willingly and unwillingly. Roughly speaking how these impasses are to be faced is clear to us from what's been said.

There is another way in which people are said to take action under compulsion or by necessity: not when thinking and yearning are discordant, but when they take actions which they consider to be painful and worthless because if they didn't do so they would be beaten or take captive or killed. They say that they take such actions by necessity; yet is that not the case, but instead they do all these things willingly? For they could refuse to do these things but suffer the consequences.

Moreover, perhaps one could sayd that some of these actions are done by necessity and some are not. In cases where it is up to a person to initiate or not initiate an action, the person always acts willingly, not under compulsion, even if doing smething unwished for; whereas in cases where it is not up to a person, the actions are in some way done under compulsion but not plain-and-simple, in that even if he is not committed to what he does he is commited to that for the sake of which he does it. But even here there is a certain difference. For if one were to kill someone to avoid being captured in a game of blind man's bluff, it would be absurd to say one did soe under compulsion and by necessity - the consequences of not taking action need to be much worse and more painful. Thus a person will take action by necessity (though not under compulsion of unaturally) when doing something bad for the sake of something good or to avoid something even worse, and one will do so unwillingly, since the circumstances are not up to him.

This is why many people set down infatuation as something unwilling, along with some fits of anger and inborn disposition, in that they have a power even beyond nature and we are forgiving of them insofar as they naturally overpower our nature. In addition, a person would seem to be acting more under compulsion and unwillingly when doing so to avoid a strong pain moreso than a mild one, and in general to avid pain than to gain delight. For it all comes down to this: what is up to oneself is a matter of what one's nature can bear; what one's nature cannot bear and what is naturally beyond yearning and thinking is not up to oneself. Hence also with those who are in the grip of inspiration and utter prophecies, we don't say it is up to them to utter what they utter or do what they do, even though they produce something that is a work of thought, nor is it something they produce through satisfying some craving. Thus some thoughts and feelings are not up to us, and neither are the actions that accord with such thoughts and reasonings. It is just as Philolaus said: some thoughts are mightier than we are.

Thus if we had to inquire into what's done willingly and unwillingly with an eye to what's done under compulsion, let them be distinguished in this way. (The accounts that especially hinder our understanding of what's done willingly are those which assert that one can act under compulsion but willingly.)
