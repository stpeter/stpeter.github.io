Aristotle's Eudemian Ethics

Book II, Chapter 10

Translated by Peter Saint-Andre

After this let us speak of commitment, first working through the impasses about it in our account. For one might be unsure about what kind of thing it naturally is and with what sorts of thing it ought to be grouped, that is, whether or not what's done willingly and what's committed to are the same thing. Most of all, some people maintain - and searching into the matter it might seem to be the case - that commitment is one or the other of two things: either opinion or yearning (for it's evident that both of these come along with commitment).

But evidently it is not yearning, for it would need to be wishing or needing or wanting, and no one yearns for something without experiencing one of these. Yet wanting and needing belong even to animals, even though they don't make commitments. Moreover, even among beings that have both of these things they often make commitments without wanting or needing; indeed their feelings are not something to which they make a commitment but something they endure. Furthermore, needing and wanting are always suffused with vexation, whereas we make many commitments without feeling vexed. However, neither are wishing and commitment the same thing, since some people wish for things they know to be impossible, such as being the king over all humankind or being immortal. By contrast, no one makes a commitment to such a thing unless ignorant of the fact that is it impossible, nor in general do people make a commitment to something that is possible unless they know it is up to them to do it or not do it. Thus it's evident that something one commits to must be something that is up to oneself.

Likewise it's manifest that commitment isn't believing, nor is it something one merely thinks. For a commitment is something that is up to oneself, whereas we form beliefs about many things that are not up to us, for instance that the diagonal of a square is proportional to its sides. Furthermore, a commitment is neither true nor false; indeed, neither is it a belief about actionable things that are up to oneself - the sort of belief by which we happen to think that we should do or not do something. This is common to both believing and wishing, for no one makes a commitment to a completion, only to that which moves one toward completion. I mean for instance no one makes a commitment to being healthy but to taking a walk or sitting down for the sake of being healthy, nor does anyone make a commitment to finding fulfillment but to transacting business or starting a venture for the sake of finding fulfillment. In general, a person who makes a commitment always manifests both what he commits to and that for the sake of which he does so: the "what for" is that for the sake of which he makes a commitment to something else, whereas the "what" is that to which he commits for the sake of something else. What he wishes for above all is the completion, and he believes that he should be healthy or do well. Thus frm these considerations it's evident that commitment is something other than believing and wishing; wishing and believing are especially about the completion, whereas commitment is not.

Thus commitment is manifestly not wishing or believing, nor is it a simple matter of taking something up for consideration. But how does it differ from these, and how does it stand in relation to what's done willingly? Answering these questions will simultaneously make manifest what commitment is.

Among things that have the capacity to be or not to be, some as such that it is possible for us to deliberate about them, whereas about others it's impossible to deliberate. The reason is that although these have the capacity to be or not to be, their coming-to-be is not up to us but instead happens due to a thing's nature or some other causes, about which no one would attempt to deliberate unless lacking in knowledge. By contrast, it is the things which not only can be or not be, but which also are subject to deliberation by human beings, that are up to us to do or not do. This is why we don't deliberate about affairs in India nor about how to square the circle, for the one is not up to us and the other is utterly unachievable. But neither do we deliberate about all things that are achievable and up to us (from which it is manifest that commitment is not believing plain-and-simple), even though the things that we commit to and act on are up to us.

For this reason one might hit an impasse about why it is that doctors deliberate regarding matters wehre they have understanding, whereas scribes do not. This is grounded in the fact that going astray can happen in two ways, for we can do so in our reasonings beforehand or with respect to our awareness while performing the act itself. It is possible for doctors to go astray in both ways, whereas scribes go astray only with respect to their awareness and activity, and if they looked into that they would go on forever.

Therefore since commitment is neither believing nor wishing, whether one of them or both together (for no one makes a commitment all of a sudden, but they can suddenly form a wish to act or a belief that they should do so), in a way it comes about from both, for both are present in someone who makes a commitment. But we must look into how it comes from both. In a way the word itself makes it clear: for commitment is pursuit, not plain-and-simple but of one thing as conducing to another, which cannot exist without looking into a matter and taking counsel. Thus commitment is the result of deliberative belief. Yet no one deliberates about what it is to be complete - that is set down for everyone - but about what aims and completin and whether this or that contributes to it - or, having come to believe that this contributes to it, deliberating about how to make it a reality. We all deliberate until we bring the starting point of making it a reality back to ourselves. No one makes a commitment without preparation and deliberation, whether they do so in a better or a worse way - deliberation about which of the things that are up to us and have the capacity to be or not to be conduce to our completion. Manifestly, then, commitment is a deliberative yearning for what is up to oneself. For we all deliberate about what we commit to, but we don't commit to all that we deliberate about. (By "deliberative" I mean that the source and cause is deliberatin and the yearning is due to having deliberated.)

This is why the other animals don't make commitments, nor do human beings in every condition or season of life - for they don't engage in deliberation or have a conception of the reasons for taking action. Although nothing prevents a belief that something should be done or not done from arising in many people, it does not arise due to reasoning. Personhood's power to deliberate is a capacity to give attention to some ground for taking action, since that for the sake of which is one of the grounds. A ground is a reason why and that for the sake of which something exists or comes to be is, we say, a ground (for instance walking to fetch one's belongings if that is the aim of walking). This is why those who have established no target don't engage in deliberation.
