Aristotle's Eudemian Ethics

Book II, Chapter 9

Translated by Peter Saint-Andre

Since our account is complete, and what's done willingly is not marked off in terms of yearning or commitment, it remains to mark off what accord with thinking things through. What's done willingly seems to be opposed to what's done unwillingly, and doing so with knowledge of the person and the means and the aim is opposed to doing so without knowledge of the person and the means and the aim not merely by-the-way but due to ignorance. (For one might know that the person is one's father but not that the result will not be saving him but killing him, as with the daughters of Pelias; or one might know a drink, i.e. the means, as wine or a love potion when it is actually hemlock.) What is done due to ignorance of the object or means or person is done unwillingly; thus the opposite is done willingly. Hence whatever one does due to oneself and not in ignorance, when it is up to oneself not to do it, must be done willingly (indeed this is what the willing is); whereas whatever is done in ignorance and due to ignorance is done unwillingly.

But there are two forms of knowing and understanding, one merely having the understanding and the other putting the understanding to use. Thus the person who has the understanding but doesn't put it into use could rightly be said to act in ignorance, but on the other hand not rightly, for instance if not putting it to use due to a lack of care. Likewise a person without the understanding could be faulted if it was straightforward or necessary to attain and their not having it was due to a lack of care or succumbing to pleasure or avoiding pain. So let these be added to our account, and let the willing and the unwilling be distinguished in this way.
